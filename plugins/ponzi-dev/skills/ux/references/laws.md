# Laws of UX: reference

All 30 laws from https://lawsofux.com/ (Jon Yablonski), paraphrased. Each entry has a definition, takeaways, and origin. Each law's page is at `https://lawsofux.com/<slug>/`.

## Aesthetic-Usability Effect (`aesthetic-usability-effect`)
People tend to see attractive designs as more usable.
- Attractive design creates positive feelings, so people assume the product works better.
- Users forgive small usability problems more readily when the design looks good.
- Polish can hide usability problems, so they may go unnoticed in testing.
- *Origin:* Kurosu and Kashimura, Hitachi Design Center, 1995 (252 participants rated ATM interfaces).

## Choice Overload (`choice-overload`)
Too many options overwhelm people (also called the paradox of choice).
- Too many options make decisions harder and lower satisfaction with the whole experience.
- When users need to compare items, show them side by side (pricing tiers, for example).
- Put the most relevant options first at each step, and offer search and filters to narrow choices early.
- *Origin:* Alvin Toffler, *Future Shock*, 1970.

## Chunking (`chunking`)
Splitting information into parts and grouping them into meaningful wholes.
- Chunked content is easier to scan, so people find what serves their goal faster.
- Visually distinct groups with clear hierarchy match how people read digital content.
- Modules, dividers, and hierarchy show how pieces of content relate.
- *Origin:* George A. Miller, 1956.

## Cognitive Bias (`cognitive-bias`)
Systematic errors in thinking that shape perception and decisions.
- Mental shortcuts save effort but can quietly skew judgment.
- Knowing about biases helps you spot them in yourself and others, though it doesn't remove them.
- Confirmation bias: people look for, interpret, and remember evidence that supports what they already believe.
- *Origin:* Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman, 1972.

## Cognitive Load (`cognitive-load`)
The mental effort needed to understand and use an interface.
- When information exceeds mental capacity, tasks get harder, details are missed, and people feel overwhelmed.
- Intrinsic load: the effort needed to hold goal-relevant information and keep track of the task.
- Extraneous load: effort wasted on distracting or unnecessary design. Minimize it.
- *Origin:* John Sweller, late 1980s (formalized 1988).

## Doherty Threshold (`doherty-threshold`)
Productivity rises when the system and the user respond to each other in under about 400 ms, so neither waits on the other.
- Aim for about 400 ms response to keep attention and momentum.
- Make waits feel shorter, even when the actual time stays the same.
- Use animation to hold attention while work happens in the background.
- Progress bars make waits tolerable, even when their estimates are rough.
- A short deliberate delay can make a result feel more considered and trustworthy.
- *Origin:* Walter J. Doherty and Ahrvind J. Thadani, IBM Systems Journal, 1982 (replaced the earlier 2-second standard).

## Fitts's Law (`fittss-law`)
The time to reach a target depends on its distance and size.
- Make touch and click targets large enough to hit accurately.
- Leave enough space between neighboring targets.
- Place targets close to where the user's attention already is.
- *Origin:* Paul Fitts, 1954.

## Flow (`flow`)
A state of energized, absorbed focus on an activity.
- Flow happens when the challenge matches the person's skill: too hard causes frustration, too easy causes boredom.
- Give feedback that shows what the user did and what it accomplished.
- Remove friction, make features discoverable, and keep the system responsive.
- *Origin:* Mihály Csíkszentmihályi, 1975.

## Goal-Gradient Effect (`goal-gradient-effect`)
Motivation rises the closer people get to a goal.
- People speed up as they near the end of a task.
- Showing artificial progress at the start increases motivation to finish.
- Clear progress indicators encourage completion.
- *Origin:* Clark Hull, 1932 (tested with rats in 1934).

## Hick's Law (`hicks-law`)
Decision time grows with the number and complexity of choices.
- Cut options when response time matters.
- Break complex tasks into smaller steps.
- Highlight a recommended option.
- Reveal features gradually to new users (progressive onboarding).
- Don't simplify so far that the design stops being clear.
- *Origin:* William Edmund Hick and Ray Hyman, 1952.

## Jakob's Law (`jakobs-law`)
Users spend most of their time on other products, so they expect yours to work the same way.
- Users carry expectations from familiar products over to similar ones.
- Using existing mental models lets users focus on their task instead of learning a new interface.
- When you redesign, reduce disruption by letting users keep the old version for a while.
- *Origin:* Jakob Nielsen (Nielsen Norman Group).

## Law of Common Region (`law-of-common-region`)
Elements inside a shared, clearly bounded area are seen as a group.
- A common region makes structure and relationships clear.
- A border around elements is an easy way to form a group.
- A shared background also marks a group.
- *Origin:* Gestalt psychology grouping principles.

## Law of Proximity (`law-of-proximity`)
Objects near each other are seen as a group.
- Closeness creates a perceived relationship.
- Nearby items are read as sharing a function or trait.
- Grouping by proximity helps users organize and process information faster.
- *Origin:* Gestalt psychology.

## Law of Prägnanz (`law-of-prägnanz`)
People perceive ambiguous or complex images in the simplest possible form, because that takes the least effort.
- The visual system prefers simplicity and order, which keeps it from being overloaded.
- Simple figures are processed and remembered better than complex ones.
- People simplify complex shapes into one unified form.
- *Origin:* Max Wertheimer, 1910 (lights at a railway crossing that looked like one moving light).

## Law of Similarity (`law-of-similarity`)
The eye groups elements that look alike, even when they are apart.
- Visually similar items are seen as related.
- Color, shape, size, orientation, and motion can signal that items belong together.
- Links and navigation must look different from plain text.
- *Origin:* Gestalt psychology.

## Law of Uniform Connectedness (`law-of-uniform-connectedness`)
Elements that are visually connected are seen as more related than unconnected ones.
- Group related items with shared color, lines, frames, or shapes.
- Connect related elements with a line or arrow.
- Use it to show context or relationships between similar items.
- *Origin:* Gestalt psychology.

## Mental Model (`mental-model`)
A person's simplified internal picture of how a system works.
- People apply what they believe about one system to similar systems.
- Designs that match users' expectations let them reuse what they already know.
- Familiar conventions work, such as the standard e-commerce listing, cart, and checkout flow.
- Close the gap between designers' and users' models through interviews, personas, journey maps, and empathy maps.
- *Origin:* Kenneth Craik, *The Nature of Explanation*, 1943.

## Miller's Law (`millers-law`)
The average person holds about 7 (±2) items in working memory.
- Don't use "seven" to justify arbitrary design limits.
- Chunk content so it is easier to process and remember.
- Short-term memory capacity varies with the person's prior knowledge and context.
- *Origin:* George Miller, 1956.

## Occam's Razor (`occams-razor`)
Among explanations that predict equally well, choose the one with the fewest assumptions.
- The best way to reduce complexity is to never add it.
- Review each element and remove as many as you can without breaking the whole.
- A design is done when nothing more can be removed.
- *Origin:* William of Ockham, c. 1287–1347.

## Paradox of the Active User (`paradox-of-the-active-user`)
Users don't read manuals; they start using the software right away.
- People want to finish their immediate task, so they skip learning up front.
- Learning the system would pay off later, but users rarely invest in it.
- Put guidance inside the workflow (contextual tooltips, inline help) so it reaches users whatever path they take.
- *Origin:* Mary Beth Rosson and John Carroll, 1987.

## Pareto Principle (`pareto-principle`)
About 80% of effects come from 20% of causes.
- Inputs and outputs are rarely distributed evenly.
- A few factors usually drive most of the result.
- Focus effort where it benefits the most users.
- *Origin:* Vilfredo Pareto (80% of Italy's land was owned by 20% of the population).

## Parkinson's Law (`parkinsons-law`)
A task expands to fill the time available for it.
- Keep task time within what users expect.
- Finishing faster than expected improves the experience.
- Autofill and prefilled details shorten forms and checkout.
- *Origin:* Cyril Northcote Parkinson, *The Economist*, 1955.

## Peak-End Rule (`peak-end-rule`)
People judge an experience mostly by its most intense moment and its end, not by the average.
- Pay extra attention to the peak moments and the end of the journey.
- Find where the product is most helpful or enjoyable and design those moments to delight.
- Negative moments are remembered more strongly than positive ones.
- *Origin:* Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber, and Redelmeier, 1993.

## Postel's Law (`postels-law`)
Be liberal in what you accept and conservative in what you send.
- Be tolerant of what users do and enter.
- Plan for varied inputs, access methods, and abilities while keeping the interface reliable and accessible.
- The more you anticipate in design, the more resilient the result.
- Accept varied input, convert it to what the system needs, set clear limits, and give clear feedback.
- *Origin:* Jon Postel (robustness principle for TCP).

## Selective Attention (`selective-attention`)
People focus on the subset of stimuli that relates to their goals.
- Users filter out what's irrelevant. Guide their attention, avoid distraction, and make relevant information easy to find.
- Banner blindness: users ignore anything that looks like an ad or sits where ads usually go. Don't make content look like ads.
- Change blindness: large changes go unnoticed without strong cues, especially when several happen at once.
- *Origin:* Donald Broadbent's filter theory, 1958.

## Serial Position Effect (`serial-position-effect`)
People remember the first and last items in a series best.
- Put less important items in the middle.
- Put key actions at the far left and right of navigation.
- *Origin:* Hermann Ebbinghaus.

## Tesler's Law (`teslers-law`)
Every system has some complexity that can't be removed (the Law of Conservation of Complexity).
- Some complexity always remains, and either the system or the user has to handle it.
- Designers and developers should take on as much of it as possible.
- Don't design for an idealized, perfectly rational user.
- Offer guidance that is easy to reach, in context, along any path.
- *Origin:* Larry Tesler, Xerox PARC, mid-1980s.

## Von Restorff Effect (`von-restorff-effect`)
Among similar items, the one that stands out is remembered best (the isolation effect).
- Make key information and primary actions visually distinct.
- Use emphasis sparingly so emphasized items don't compete or look like ads.
- Don't rely on color alone; people with color blindness or low vision may miss it.
- Use motion carefully, since it bothers people who are sensitive to movement.
- *Origin:* Hedwig von Restorff, 1933.

## Working Memory (`working-memory`)
The mental system that briefly holds and works with the information a task needs.
- It holds about 4 to 7 chunks, and each fades after about 20 to 30 seconds. Show only what is needed.
- Recognition is easier than recall. Mark what users have already seen (visited links, breadcrumbs).
- Let the system carry information between screens (comparison tables, for example) instead of the user's memory.
- *Origin:* Miller, Galanter, and Pribram, 1960s; adopted by Atkinson and Shiffrin, 1968.

## Zeigarnik Effect (`zeigarnik-effect`)
People remember unfinished or interrupted tasks better than finished ones.
- Use clear signs that more content is available to invite exploration.
- Showing some early progress makes people more likely to finish.
- Show users how far they've come.
- *Origin:* Bluma Zeigarnik, 1920s.
